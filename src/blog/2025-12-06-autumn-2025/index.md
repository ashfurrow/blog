[I recently found myself a new job](https://ashfurrow.com/blog/joining-wealthsimple/), and opted to take two weeks off between positions. But we're not here to talk about work, we're here to talk about photography!

Landscapes have never been a strength of mine but New Brunswick is filled with lots of beautiful scenes for practice. I treated the two weeks between jobs as a kind of photography workshop, with the aim to get out everyday with a camera. I had been collecting a list of interesting locales, researching places online – but sometimes I would just _go_ without a specific destination.

Here are my favourite photos. The sections are chronologically ordered, so you can see changes in the leaves. Maybe changes in my photographic approach, too.

## Kelly Creek Lookout

This was a bit of a trek. I came across an online discussion of local places to watch the sunset, and Kelly Creek Lookout came was strongly recommended. "Drive until the road gets too bad, then walk the rest of the way up the mountain." It was worth it.

My wife and would have stayed longer, but after sunset it got dark _very_ quickly. Even with a flashlight, it was a rocky downhill slope to get back to the car.

## Marysville

Another thing you might notice are the different... tones, I guess? of these photos. I experimented a lot with different "film simulations" built into my Fujifilm camera. The one below is "Nostalgic Negative", but I don't know what specific film stock it's based on. I like the shadows here, it's very muted.

![](10.jpg#wide)

I also tried "Vivid" (which is based on Velvia slide film) and "Pro Hi" (which is based on Pro 400H). I actually _have_ shot on both of these films, but _mostly_ Pro 400H. [I once (accidentally) brought slide film with me to Verona](/blog/verona/), and I found it very difficult to expose for. But, when I got the exposure right, the results were stunning.

Pro 400H was actually my favourite film stock, before Fujifilm discontinued it. It was saturated but only in ways that complimented my photography – I don't think it'd be great at portraiture.

I like how these different film simulations looked, and it was fun to compare them. My camera actually has "film simulation bracketing", where it will save the exposure in three different film simulations. It seemed like that would be more flexible, since you could pick the one you liked the most later. I tried this, but really disliked having to sort through triple the images. I actually _liked_ choosing a "mood" _in camera_, instead of shooting raw and deciding later. It felt like choosing a film stock. And just like film, where you wish you'd have used different film, there are a few shots I look back on and wish I'd used a different film simulation.

In my two weeks, I shot over two thousand photos. Frankly, I'm getting tired of editing. I can do it, but editing from raw every time is tedious. I don't think I'm a full-time JPEG shooter, but it has become my default.

## Marysville Flats

One afternoon, I had this magical experience, like I was in a video game. Like I had stumbled across a side quest from _Zelda: Breath of the Wild_. I was walking on a trail, alongside a field, and I saw a billboard in the corner of that field. So, I left the trail, crossed the field, and ~~chopped the sign down~~ read the signage.

The billboard turned out to mark the entrance to the Marysville Flats, a nature restoration project to _plant a forrest_ of Silver Maple trees. I followed goat trails through the forrest, which would occasionally spit me out at various points along the Nashwaak River. I returned the next day for more photos, and I will be back again.

The below photo set is when I switched from "mostly Pro 400H" to "mostly Vivid" film simulations. Things get more colourful from here.

## Falling Barn

Another entry on my list of local locations to photograph. It's hard to stop your car on the side of a country road to take photos of someone's barn, _without_ seeming like a creep. My approach in these situations is to seem like an obvious photography nerd, which comes naturally to me.

Like with street photography, I try to avoid making people feel uncomfortable. But unlike street photography, where I try to blend in to a scene, I intentionally tried to stick out.

To the owner of this barn: I'm sorry your roof was torn off last winter, but I'm grateful you didn't fix it. I'd been looking forward to taking these photos for months, and I think your barn is beautiful.

## Priceville Footbridge

This is another spot that I saw recommended online. The Priceville Footbridge spans the Miramichi River, which is _very_ low in these photos due to a historic drought. I mention the unusually low water because this footbridge has a deadly history, once being swept away by the river in 1939. There is a sign describing the story, and I'm glad I only read that sign _after_ I'd crossed the footbridge twice.

After the accident, the bridge was rebuilt with a midway support embedded in the riverbed (the footbridge had been spanning the entire river, which, yikes). However, that is not to suggest that this footbridge is safe. This bridge _got me_, by which I mean, its side wall scraped a six-inch cut into my left arm as I brushed by it. (This is why it's important to keep a first aide kit in your car.)

I'm still not great a sunsets. I think I'm exposing for the highlights still. The problem is, sunsets are hard to do with big cameras.

Okay hear me out. Smartphones embody the "computation photography" approach to making good photos, to "just make this scene look pretty" using algorithms and clever image signal processing. "Big cameras" with dials and controls and whatnot embody the "let me make the decisions" approach. I find it hard to make the right decisions to take a pretty sunset photo, even if it's easy to take a good sunset photo on my smartphone.

Frankly, I don't want to mess with stitching bracketed exposures into HDR photos, or whatever else, when shooting on my big camera. I want the control, without the faffing, but I'm not yet consistently getting the results I'm looking for. This is a work in progress.

## Eel River Lake

This hill and I go way back. I _wish_ I could take a photo that conveys what it's like to drive over this hill. It feels like a dolly zoom, as stacked layers of trees swoop down around you then fall away. As the car crests over the top of the hill, you feel gravity get turned down for just a moment before smashing you down into your seat. Maybe its just childhood memories I have – and those are hard to capture in a photo.

I found myself driving over this hill again so I stopped the car, and tried to take a photo of this feeling.

It didn't really work. Taking photos while _standing in the middle of the road_ _<u>on a blind hill</u>_ is very dangerous, so I couldn't quite get the effect I was going for. Maybe I'll try again someday.
