---
title: LinkedIn for AI
date: 2026-11-10
---

My corner of the web was atwitter lately as a [few](https://marco.org/2026/10/24/community) prominent [members](https://daringfireball.net/2026/10/x_once_again_marks_the_spot) of the Apple/iOS developer community decided to announce they were spending time on X again; [people got mad](https://lexontech.org/i-cant-believe-were-still-arguing-over-whether-x-is-for-jerks). And it's kind of bothering me how people seem to be talking past each other about it.

The people who rejoined X characterized it as a nice place to be, with enthusiastic users. (Maybe? For white guys I guess? Some brown folks on X have different experiences.) I don't know for sure, but I'm guessing that the users on X aren't really enthusiastic _about_ the community there, they're enthusiastic about AI. The returners to X started posting about AI immediately, so, yeah I think there's something to this draw to X based on interest in AI.

No one seems mad at the people who stayed on X, only at the ones who returned. (Edit: [Ole points out that this isn't necessarily true](https://chaos.social/@ole/117598468984901718); it's more accurate for me to say that _I_ don't see complaints about people who stayed on X.) We all see what we want to see. A lot of people (myself included) left Twitter to find a new online home for text-based social media. When a member of that new home departs, and tells us that the original place we all left is _actually nice now_, it can feel threatening. Especially since, you know, it's obviously not true. Twitter is dead. The "[Salad Days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salad_days) of Twitter" will never, ever return.

A lot of iOS developers left Twitter to join the fediverse. For context, [I created `mastodon.technology` in 2017](/blog/mastodon/) and by the time [I shut it down in 2022](https://ashfurrow.com/blog/mastodon-technology-shutdown/), it had grown to one of the top-ten largest Mastodon servers on the Fediverse. I still have a lot of friends there. But I hardly _ever_ post about technology anymore because what I'm most excited about these days is agentic coding. And I know what the reaction would be if I tooted about it.

So. I'm _guessing_ that the people who have rejoined X felt the same way. They didn't feel able to discuss AI without naysayers so they left, to return to _the_ place on the web where enthusiasm for AI is the norm. Where the people _creating_ agentic coding tools hang out. Where the builders _using_ agentic tools discuss their techniques.

X is LinkedIn for AI.

And I mean that derisively. LinkedIn is widely, correctly seen as a fake place. Somewhere to aura farm professional success points. "What getting divorced taught me about B2B sales." There is _technically_ community on LinkedIn, but, not in the sense that a lot of people are looking for. It's inauthentic. I tried scrolling on X the other day, and I felt that inauthenticity. Not every post, not as uniformly. But it just feels... fake. In a similar way that LinkedIn feels fake.

...but LinkedIn can be useful. I post to LinkedIn and no one gets mad. If the people rejoining X had been upfront about joining _to discuss AI_ then maybe everyone wouldn't have gotten mad. Or, maybe they've have gotten even madder; people _do_ hate AI.

And not for no reason, either.

This is complicated.

[I have mixed feelings](/blog/foresight/). Hoovering up all the internet's data to train LLMs was not something anyone consented to. But I _did_ release _a lot_ of open source software under an MIT license, and this blog is licensed under the Creative Commons. So I _did_ consent. People are mad at data centres using up water and electricity, but text-based inference is one of the least resource-intensive uses of AI. And I'm on the record that [agentic coding tools can continue to improve without models getting better](/blog/ai-coding-tools/). Anthropic wants to keep spending resources on LLM training, to create the machine god. Okay? But I'm just trying to make apps. And _that_ doesn't use a lot of resources.

Some friends of mine would put the genie back in the bottle, so to speak. If they could, they'd wave a magic wand and programmers would all go back to standup updates like "yesterday I renamed a variable, today I'm renaming it back." [I would not](/blog/agency). At some point in 2026, I crossed the threshold where I'm more excited than I am nervous about agentic coding. That shift has let me to be more curious about the tooling, and want to learn more. I feel so powerful, able to leverage my deep expertise far more than I could without the tools.

I avoid X because it feels _gross_ (even before but especially since Twitter's acquisition). And because its owner _sucks_. However, I must admit that any time I've logged onto X over the past two years, one first recommended posts I see has had some tip or technique that _significantly_ improved how I used agentic coding tools. Like, it's not even funny. I'd log in to look at something a coworker sent me (you have to log in these days) and I'd inadvertently see the home screen, and I'd stop in my tracks. "Holy shit, that's a good idea."

The cost I pay to avoid X is having to seek out secondary sources for AI tips and tricks on Slack or YouTube. Doesn't seem so bad right? Well, like I said, I'm excited about these tools. Everything I see has been filtered. I don't get to see the ideas _as they're forming_. I only see the ideas that get notoriety, never the weird experiments. Never the failures. Getting all my information second-hand means I don't have as clear a picture of where the tools are going. That's not the end of the world, but it is an opportunity cost.

Recently, someone shared the [herdr Desktop](https://herdr.dev) app on Slack just after it was released. It looked so cool! So I tried it out right away. I'd been planning to build myself some custom tooling anyway and herdr turned out to be the _perfect_ platform to start from. I was one of the first people at work to try it out and I shared what I learned. Other developers got excited by what I'd built with it so we got together to exchange workflows. It was the first time I _felt_ ahead of the curve with agentic coding tools.

You can be excited for AI. I am. You can avoid X. I do. The more closely I follow the advancements in agentic coding, the more that choice costs me. I've had to find quality secondary sources. You don't _need_ a LinkedIn account, and you don't _need_ an X account either. Even for AI.
